Workshop with José A. Pons · neutron-star cooling · preliminary results

Can slow cooling explain three cold young neutron stars?

A first check, with our own 1D code, of Marino et al. 2024 (Nat. Astron. 8, 1020). Hypotheses were written down before each calculation, and every number on this page is inserted from the result files when the page is built.

In brief

  • The paper’s main claim holds so far. A standard slow-cooling star is 13 to 35 times brighter than the three sources. We tried 9 proton and 8 neutron superfluidity models, alone and combined, at six masses and with two versions of the pair-breaking emission rate. None of them reaches any source. The closest case is still 2.0 times too bright.
  • One sentence of the paper is too strong. It says superfluidity accelerates cooling unless gap choices are extremely unusual. In our sweep 50% to 77% of the combinations are colder than the plain star; the rest are warmer, and they are ordinary published models.
  • A detail worth knowing. In BSk24 the fast channel opens at 1.595 M☉. The paper’s 1.6 M☉ model is therefore only 0.005 M☉ above the threshold, and the classifier assigns about 1.6 M☉ to both pulsars.
  • This is not the final word. The interior is treated as one uniform temperature, effective masses are fixed at 0.7, there is no crust physics, and only SLy4 has been swept. The list of what is missing is below.
  • Checks: 41 of 46 pass. The 5 failures are listed and explained; none was fixed by changing a criterion.

The question

Three young neutron stars are much fainter than expected for their age. The paper concludes that ordinary slow cooling cannot explain them, so dense matter must allow a fast cooling channel (direct Urca or hyperons), and that this would exclude most current equations of state.

That conclusion rests on 81 runs of a 2D magneto-thermal code with three equations of state, one of them without a fast channel (SLy4), and a single superfluidity model. José asked for an independent look with a simple 1D code and updated microphysics. We split the paper’s argument into seven statements and test them one at a time.

What this means. Superfluidity lowers the luminosity by a factor of about 5 to 5 at best, through the extra neutrino emission when neutron pairs form. That is not enough. The boosted version of the main slow channel (Shternin, Baldo & Haensel 2018) dims the plain star by a factor 1.5 to 1.6, but barely moves the best superfluid cases, because there that channel is already suppressed.

A caveat for the “75 % excluded” figure. The boost grows sharply for equations of state that come close to the fast-cooling threshold without crossing it. In SLy4 it is modest (a factor 2.8 to 4.9 over the bare rate’s normalisation, against 5 to 10 quoted by its authors for their own model) because SLy4 never approaches the threshold. SLy4 may therefore be the least favourable kind of slow-cooling star. We have not tested other equations of state yet.

  • The two stars are the paper’s stars. BSk24: maximum mass 2.279 M☉, fast channel above 8.25×10¹⁴ g/cm³. SLy4: maximum mass 2.050 M☉ and radius 9.99 km, as in Douchin & Haensel 2001.
  • The heat budget is right. Stored heat within 3.9% and the simplest neutrino channel within 13.0% of the published fits for SLy4 (their own margins: 12 % and 54 %).
  • The standard cooling curve is right for light and medium stars. Surface temperature at 330 years: 1.43 to 1.47 million K, against about 1.4 in the reference.
  • The superfluidity formulas are transcribed correctly. They reproduce their limiting forms and the peak of a published figure (ours 1.10×10²¹ erg cm⁻³ s⁻¹, read off the figure as 10²¹).

What is not covered yet

  • Uniform interior temperature. Doubtful for the 841-year source. Needs the full heat-flow version (statement S4).
  • Effective masses. Fixed at 0.7 of the bare mass, as in the references we validated against. Realistic values are the next ingredient.
  • The crust. No crust neutrino emission, heat capacity or neutron singlet superfluid.
  • Mass range. All the best cases sit at the lowest mass tried (1.0 M☉), the edge of the grid.
  • Other equations of state. Only SLy4 has been swept. Models near the fast-cooling threshold could behave differently.
  • Fast cooling itself. Direct Urca is not in the code yet, so we have not reproduced the paper’s BSk24 and GM1A curves.
  • Combined reduction factors. We use the approximate rules of Yakovlev et al. 2001, and assume the same factors apply to the boosted channel.
  • The high-mass discrepancy in the standard cooling benchmark (table above) is unresolved.

Questions for José

  1. Which statements of the paper do you consider not rigorous? Do they match S1–S7?
  2. Which gap model of Ho et al. 2015 did the paper use? And which one in the hyperon runs?
  3. The 1.6 M☉ BSk24 model is 0.005 M☉ above the direct-Urca threshold. Was that known when the classifier assigned about 1.6 M☉ to both pulsars?
  4. Which ingredients do you consider outdated, beyond the ones tried here (gaps, pair-breaking rate, in-medium modified Urca)?
  5. Which equations of state without direct Urca would you add? In particular, ones that come close to the threshold.
  6. Could we have the paper’s cooling curves as data (at least SLy4 and BSk24), to compare number by number?

Reproduce it

Code, results and the lab notebook are in the ns_cooling folder of the repository. Third-party files (the equation-of-state fits and the papers) are not redistributed; ns_cooling/README.md says where to get them.

uv run python -m ns_cooling.step3_integrals
